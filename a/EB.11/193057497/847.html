SOPHIST, an appellation assumed in the early pe-
riods of Grecian history by those who devoted their
time to the study of science. This appellation appear-
ing too arrogant to Pythagoras, he declined it, and
wished to be called a philosopher; declaring that, though
he could not consider himself as a wise man, he was in-
deed a lover of wisdom. True wisdom and modesty are
generally

generally united. The example of Pythagoras was followed by every man of eminence; while the name sophist was retained only by those who with a pomp of words made a magnificent display of wisdom upon a very slight foundation of knowledge. Those men taught an artificial structure of language, and a false method of reasoning, by which, in argument, the worse might be made to appear the better reason (see SOPHISM). In Athens they were long held in high repute, and supported, not only by contributions from their pupils, but by a regular salary from the state. They were among the bitterest enemies of the illustrious Socrates, because he embraced every opportunity of exposing to contempt and ridicule their vain pretensions to superior knowledge, and the pernicious influence of their doctrines upon the taste and morals of the Athenian youth.
